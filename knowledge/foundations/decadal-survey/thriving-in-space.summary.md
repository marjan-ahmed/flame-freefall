"Thriving in Space" is the National Academies' 2023-2032 decadal survey for
biological and physical sciences in space. This entry covers only its
combustion/fire-safety content, not the full 322-page book.

- Combustion science matters doubly: it's fundamental research (86% of world
  energy demand runs on combustion) *and* it directly informs spacecraft fire
  safety.
- States the corpus's core motivation directly: Artemis's lower-pressure,
  higher-O₂ cabin atmospheres mean ISS-derived fire safety practices "may no
  longer offer the same factor of safety" — and existing studies "have been
  limited in terms of flame and fire sizes, diagnostics, pressures, and the
  effects of lunar and martian gravity."
- Traces the flammability-index question (MOC/LOI vs. the NASA-STD-6001B
  pass/fail test) back to the *previous* (2011) decadal survey — this is a
  decade-old, still-open gap, not a new one.
- Names BASS, FLEX, and ACME directly — FLEX and ACME together showed the
  first evidence of two-stage "cool flame" combustion behavior.
- **Box 2-6** gives the full cool-flame story: an accidental ISS discovery,
  self-sustaining for up to 40 seconds, studied via FLEX's own MDCA/CIR
  hardware at 0.5–3 atm — with direct relevance to terrestrial low-temperature
  engine combustion (HCCI, RCCI).
- Names NASA-STD-6001B's own known drawbacks explicitly: it's a pass/fail
  ground test that ignores that spacecraft cabin conditions can change, that
  microgravity flow is HVAC/astronaut-driven (low, irregular velocity, no
  natural convection), and that combustion products don't disperse the way
  they would under buoyancy.
- A "Chemical Kinetics and Combustion Processes" priority area ties radiative
  loss, cool/warm flames, soot super-agglomerate formation, and electric-field
  effects on flames together — directly connecting to ACME's CLD Flame and
  E-FIELD Flames sub-experiments by name.
