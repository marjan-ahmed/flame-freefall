Fletcher Miller's decadal-survey white paper questions whether NASA's
ground-based flammability certification (NASA-STD-6001B Test #1) is actually
conservative once real microgravity/partial-gravity data exists.

- Test #1 assumes Earth's buoyancy-driven upward flame spread is the
  worst case — but "over the last decade... this is sometimes not the case,"
  especially for materials that differ from what's actually flown.
- BASS/BASS-II ran 70+ papers' worth of tests but was capped at 1 atm and
  <22% O₂ by MSG hardware — not the up-to-34% O₂ atmospheres proposed for
  future exploration missions.
- 1g vs. 0g/partial-g flammability-limit differences are **largest at Lunar
  gravity**, indicating "poor safety margins" there — the paper gives a
  physical mechanism: Lunar buoyancy may pump just enough oxidizer into the
  flame zone to sustain burning without blowing it off.
- Saffire (5 flights by this paper's writing, samples 1-2 orders of
  magnitude larger than before) found fires can keep burning after
  ventilation is cut, and can even ignite or extinguish neighboring samples —
  the ISS's standard first response isn't guaranteed to work.
- Upcoming SoFIE/CIR facility (2022) runs 5 experiments (GEL, MIST, SMURF,
  RTDFS, NCA) at up to 34% O₂ — closer to future exploration atmospheres than
  MSG ever reached.
- Lists 8 concrete research needs for the coming decade: partial-gravity
  material testing, real (not idealized) materials at exploration
  atmospheres, long-duration thick-material testing, new low-flammability
  materials/coatings, configuration studies, battery fire research, a new
  gravity-aware flammability standard, and higher-throughput screening tests.
- One genuine gap: the paper's Table 1 (ULOI/MOC by gravity level) and Figure
  2 (flammability map) appear to be image-only in the source PDF — their
  numeric/visual content didn't convert to text and isn't in this corpus yet.
