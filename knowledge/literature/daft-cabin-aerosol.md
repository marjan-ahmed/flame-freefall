> [!NOTE]
> DAFT describes background aerosols and instrument feasibility, not a set
> of identified combustion tests.

## Why the experiment was needed

Spacecraft fire detection must distinguish a fire signature from ordinary
background particles and nuisance sources. The presentation explains that
detector design therefore needs information about both smoke and the aerosol
environment in which a detector operates. Its discussion of microgravity smoke
concerns particle growth and detector response; the cabin measurements described
below concern the background against which a fire would need to be detected.
Those are related measurement problems, but they are not interchangeable
experimental results.[^daft-2007]

DAFT means **Dust Aerosol Measurement Feasibility Test**. Its immediate motivation
was uncertainty about the low-gravity operation of the P-Trak: control of
condensate in its condenser was not assured. The presentation describes a
rapid-turnaround experiment to investigate that problem. Establishing that an
instrument functions in low gravity is different from establishing its
calibration, detection limit or discrimination between smoke and other
particles.[^daft-2007]

## Instruments and flight sequence

The source identifies a P-Trak and a Dust Trak flown on Expedition 10, followed
by a reference source on Expedition 13. It presents the work in separate
results groups, which must remain separate when interpreting the evidence.
The later reference-dust results are not additional measurements of undisturbed
cabin air.[^daft-2007]

| Source results group | Activity | What the presentation reports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DAFT 1-2 | Sampling undisturbed cabin air | Measurements at locations in the lab and node, with bounds on average instrument readings |
| DAFT 1-2 | Separating Velcro | Deliberately generated aerosol; a time-history plot is shown |
| DAFT 3-4 | Sampling a reference dust source | Comparison of the instruments over 3 runs, with an alcohol-supply limitation |

The cabin-average Dust Trak result is expressed as mass concentration, whereas
the P-Trak result is expressed as particle number concentration. These are
different measured quantities. The source does not supply a conversion between
them that could turn the pair into a particle-size distribution or a common
smoke-concentration scale.[^daft-2007]

## Undisturbed cabin air

The DAFT 1-2 slide states that undisturbed air was sampled at “2 locations in
the lab and in the node.” It also identifies a test in which aerosol was
deliberately generated by separating Velcro. The presentation calls the cabin
substantially cleaner than the shuttle or a typical office environment. That
comparison is the presentation's campaign-level characterization; it is not a
numerical ratio or an assertion about every cabin location or operating
condition.[^daft-2007]

| Instrument | Reported average cabin-air bound | Quantity represented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dust Trak | less than 0.005 mg/m3 | Instrument-reported mass concentration |
| P-Trak | less than 15 particles /cm3 | Instrument-reported particle number concentration |

The wording “average values of cabin air less than” applies to these reported
campaign averages. It does not establish that each instantaneous reading was
below the bound, that the bounds are instrument detection limits, or that the
same values would hold during a deliberately generated aerosol event. No exact
point estimate is substituted for either reported bound.[^daft-2007]

## Deliberately generated aerosol and plotted time histories

The source includes a “Velcro separation” time history and a separate
“Measurements in the Lab” slide. These make the distinction between an
intentional particle-generating activity and background measurements visible.
The plot labels identify time and instrument-reading units, but the accompanying
text does not provide a sample-by-sample numerical table or an enumerated
association between each plotted point and a sampling record. Axis labels alone
are not measurements of a peak, duration or event-averaged concentration.
Consequently, those quantities are not reconstructed here from the text
extraction.[^daft-2007]

The broader presentation also discusses smoke detectors, smoke experiments and
the planned SAME work. Those sections provide motivation and context. They do
not make the Velcro event a combustion test, and their smoke-particle results
must not be attributed to DAFT's cabin or reference-dust measurements merely
because the slides appear in the same presentation.[^daft-2007]

## Reference-dust comparison and instrument feasibility

The DAFT 3-4 slide describes sampling a reference dust source for **3 runs**.
Its stated result is that the data compared well for the first run, before
effects of low alcohol became significant. The slide concludes that the
functionality of the modified P-Trak in low gravity was demonstrated.
The low-alcohol qualification belongs beside that conclusion: the source does
not say that agreement remained equally good throughout the later runs.
The labels “Sample Bag 1,” “Sample Bag 2” and “Sample Bag 3” identify traces in
the comparison, but do not by themselves provide uncertainty estimates or a
quantitative agreement criterion.[^daft-2007]

This is evidence of instrument feasibility under the reported conditions.
Neither a calibration curve, a guaranteed response to all dust compositions,
nor a validated smoke-versus-dust classifier is supplied by that statement.
The comparison also cannot establish a material-specific combustion outcome:
the reported source is reference dust, not a burning specimen.[^daft-2007]

## What the source answers, and what remains open

| Question | Answer supported by this presentation | Remaining evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why was low-gravity feasibility investigated? | Condensate control in the P-Trak condenser was not assured. | The modification specification and qualification procedure would be needed to reproduce the instrument configuration. |
| Were cabin background aerosols measured? | Yes; undisturbed lab and node air was sampled, and bounds on average readings are reported. | Identified sampling intervals, instrument states and location-resolved numerical records are needed for a time-resolved comparison. |
| Was an intentional nuisance source observed? | Yes; the source identifies aerosol generated by separating Velcro. | A numerical event record is needed before reporting a peak or integrated aerosol exposure. |
| Did the reference-dust measurements support feasibility? | The source reports good comparison in the first run and demonstrated modified-instrument functionality in low gravity. | The dust specification, agreement metric and alcohol-state record are needed to quantify performance and interpret later runs. |
| Do the reported bounds define a fire alarm setting? | No alarm threshold or detection-limit determination is supplied by the cabin-average statement. | A detector-specific calibration and discrimination study would be needed. |

These open questions identify the records needed for further quantitative use.
They do not mean that the experiment failed or that no additional records exist;
they mark what this particular presentation establishes and what it does not.
