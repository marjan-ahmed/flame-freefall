## Background


Flame spread behavior, flammability, and suppression data are of interest to NASA for
application to fire safety efforts. The reduced buoyant flows, coupled with low-speed air
circulation currents, create an environment quite different than normal Earth gravity. In
addition, there are several oxidizer atmospheres proposed for crew-manned vehicles
and habitats [NASA CxP-70024], which further complicates the problem.
Understanding flammability and suppression in a variety of environments is especially
important given NASA's current exploration initiatives.


There is some evidence for the hypothesis that materials in microgravity, with adequate
ventilation, burn as well if not better than the same material in normal gravity with other
conditions being identical (pressure, oxygen concentration, temperature, etc.) To test
this hypothesis with any but the thinnest materials requires extended microgravity time -
longer than available in drop towers or parabolic aircraft. If this hypothesis is true, it
implies serious safety concerns over the utility and margin of safety provided NASA-
STD-6001 Test #1.


There are important differences in the suppression of fires in space compared to on
Earth. On Earth it is understood that best results are generally obtained when the
extinguisher "attacks" the base of the flame, which is both the stabilization point and the
point of fresh air entrainment. For a fire burning in microgravity, the best point of
application of suppressant may not be immediately apparent, especially for a partially
obstructed flame or a wake-stabilized flame. Depending on the geometry of the flame
and the characteristics of the extinguisher (distance from flame, dispersion angle) it is
possible that the suppressant stream will be ineffective or might actually make the flame
worse through the entrainment of oxygen.


# Objectives


There are two main objectives:


1.) Begin to bridge the gap between the normal gravity NASA-STD-6001 Test#1


method, ground-based microgravity tests, and actual material flammability in space.
The relation between these can be investigated by observing flames burning for the
longer times available in flight. Practical materials in typical geometries will be
examined, including wake flames which have characteristics of a fire burning behind
another object (for example, a fire burning in tight area which may not be easily
accessible). Fuels can be significantly thicker than the thin sheets studied in
ground-based facilities.

# Hardware changes


The plan is to use the existing NASA glovebox SPICE hardware with four changes:


1. Accommodation of replaceable solid fuel samples

2. Igniter element adjustable (e.g. by bending wire leads) into the correct position

3. Nitrogen gas jet for flame extinguishment

4. Providing a way to shut off the flow completely

The air co-flow is produced by using a fan to blow air through the duct. An anemometer
is used to measure the actual flow speed. The airflow speed can be adjusted from 5 to
50 cm/s. Nitrogen is supplied through the nozzle from pressurized bottles via a mass
flow controller, from 0 to 500 cc/min. A radiometer measures flame output. Video and
still cameras are used to image the flame.

For the solid spheres, flame growth rate, flame shape, and luminosity are the main
interests. Again, the suppression system will be engaged after some period of burning.
Some of these samples will have inert hemispheres so that a wake flame can be
investigated (the inert hemispheres prevent flames from spreading into the forward-
stagnation region). Wake flames may occur in real situations where flames are
confined behind obstacles and within tight spaces. The burning behavior of these
samples and their suppression characteristics are of interest. For the candles within
tubes, the effect of suppression amount on the flame structure at different flow speeds
will be investigated.
