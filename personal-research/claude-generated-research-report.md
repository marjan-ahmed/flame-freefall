! **Buried Embers: A Problem-Discovery Report on Why NASA's Microgravity Combustion Data Is Hard 
to Find, Compare, and Understand**

## TL;DR
- **The problem is real, named, and current.** Specific NASA Glenn combustion scientists and university PIs must manually pull decades of microgravity fire data — spread across dozens of separate investigations, PDFs, journal papers, and an incomplete online repository — into hand-built comparison tables before they can answer basic questions. NASA's own Decadal Survey white papers say this fragmentation slows fire-safety work for Artemis and Mars.
- **The root cause is structural, not accidental.** Each mission (BASS, FLEX, SAFFIRE, ACME, etc.) was individually scoped, funded, and closed out; findings live mainly in narrative PDFs and papers rather than standardized machine-readable data; and the central repository (Physical Sciences Informatics, PSI) explicitly omits combustion investigations from before ~2000 and was never funded as a unification effort.
- **The cost when unsolved is documented in NASA sources.** Spacecraft designers have been "forced to base their designs and safety precautions on 1-g understanding"; material certification (NASA-STD-6001B Test 1) may under-predict flammability at lunar gravity; and researchers say the difficulty of assembling prior data is a barrier to model validation and to applying modern analysis.

## Key Findings
1. **Named people have this problem today.** Ya-Ting Liao (Case Western Reserve University) holds an active NASA grant whose core task is literally to pull BASS, BASS-II, and SAFFIRE-I/II/III data out of the PSI repository and hand-process it so that flame characteristics "will be extracted, documented, and compared." Sandra Olson, Paul Ferkul, David Urban, Gary Ruff (NASA Glenn), James T'ien (CWRU), Subrata Bhattacharjee (SDSU), and Fletcher Miller (SDSU) are recurring investigators whose papers repeatedly perform manual microgravity-vs-normal-gravity comparisons. Spacecraft materials engineers applying NASA-STD-6001B are the downstream decision-makers.
2. **The scale is large and heterogeneous.** NASA's PSI History page states NASA "launched the PSI database in 2014 with 17 datasets"; the 2021 NTRS paper "What's New in the PSI?" notes growth "to 72 investigations available with 8 new datasets in the 2020 NRA (NNH20ZDA014N)." Combustion science alone spans roughly 20 distinct investigations.
3. **The formats are inconsistent.** FLEX alone reported its "first 284 tests"; BASS-II ran ~100 fuel samples and its facility produced "70 conference and journal articles"; SAFFIRE-I downlinked 25 gigabits of data comprising 40,989 images and data files. Results live as PDFs, journal articles, conference proceedings, NASA Technical Memoranda, raw image/video files, and PSI records — with no common schema.
4. **Root cause is structural fragmentation.** NASA's Decadal Survey white paper (Liao et al.) states the PSI "is far from completion," that "combustion investigations before the 2000s are missing," and that diagnostic inconsistency "prevent[s] direct comparisons between different experiments." Each mission was a separate line item; PSI was created in 2014 as an open-science archive, not a standardization program.
5. **Cost is sourced.** The SAFFIRE VI paper states plainly that "the lack of this experimental data has forced spacecraft designers to base their designs and safety precautions on 1-g understanding." The Decadal materials white paper (Miller et al.) says the assumption that ground testing is conservative "is sometimes not the case," with the worst safety margins at Lunar gravity.
6. **Prior partial fixes exist but stop short.** Fujita's 2015 review and several comparison papers synthesize slices of the data; Liao's grant is building a MATLAB pipeline to categorize PSI data — but it is one PI, focused on BASS/BASS-II/SAFFIRE, still manual, and explicitly does not cover pre-2000 experiments or the full combustion corpus.

## Details

### Layer 1 — Who actually has the problem

Not "NASA" in the abstract. The people are identifiable:

- **The combustion PI reconciling old and new runs.** Ya-Ting Liao (Case Western Reserve University) holds an active NASA Physical Sciences Informatics grant (NASA Task Book id=17602, FY2026, updated 10/08/2025), titled *"Predicting Material Flammability in Partial Gravity using Microgravity and Ground Data."* Its stated work is to develop code "to analyze data available on the NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) system for microgravity combustion experiments (Burning and Suppression of Solids/BASS, BASS-II; Spacecraft Fire Safety Experiments: SAFFIRE-I, SAFFIRE-II, and SAFFIRE-III)," from which "Flame characteristics including spread rate, length, appearance, color, and flammability boundaries will be extracted, documented, and compared." Her progress reports describe "literature review, categorization of the PSI data, and development of a MATLAB code to process the PSI data." This is the anchor problem in operational form: a PI hand-building the cross-experiment comparison because it does not already exist.

- **The materials/flammability decision-maker.** Spacecraft materials engineers qualify materials by NASA-STD-6001B Test 1, described in the Decadal materials white paper as "an ignition and upward flame spread test in normal Earth gravity at the worst end-use ambient oxygen concentration and pressure." That same paper (Fletcher Miller, SDSU, with Sandra Olson and Michael Johnston, NASA Glenn) documents that these engineers must also grant "waivers" for flammable items (clothing, cotton towels, Delrin camera mounts, paper notebooks) and that the flammability boundary at Lunar gravity shows "poor safety margins." NASA Glenn's FM2 page adds that NASA-STD-6001B's 1-g test is one "which some researchers believe is not conservative in low-g."

- **The early-career researcher / grad student building on prior work.** Task Book id=17602 names Ph.D. student Jiaxuan Han running simulations of SAFFIRE VI using "data available in the PSI." The pattern recurs across CWRU, SDSU, and UC Berkeley student authors (Ama Carney, Yanjun Li, Chengyao Li, Wohan Cui, Luca Carmignani) whose papers begin by re-collecting scattered prior microgravity data.

- **The recurring named investigators.** Sandra Olson, Paul Ferkul, David Urban, Gary Ruff (NASA Glenn); James T'ien (CWRU); Subrata Bhattacharjee (SDSU); Osamu Fujita (Hokkaido); Fletcher Miller (SDSU); and Daniel Dietrich (Glenn's discipline science lead for the Microgravity Combustion Science Program). Their titles are explicit about manual comparison — e.g., Bhattacharjee, Laue, Carmignani, Ferkul, Olson, *"Opposed-flow flame spread: A comparison of microgravity and normal gravity experiments to establish the thermal regime"* (Fire Safety Journal, 2016).

### Layer 2 — What it looks like in practice

The concrete pain is assembling scattered, heterogeneous results by hand before any comparison can begin.

**Counts and span:**
- PSI launched in 2014 with 17 datasets and grew to 72 investigations across six disciplines (biophysics, combustion science, complex fluids/soft matter, fluid physics, fundamental physics, materials science). Combustion science contains roughly 20 named investigations: ACME BRE, ACME E-FIELD Flames, ACME S-Flames, BASS, BASS-II, CFI (Cool Flames Investigation), DAFT, DAFT-2, FLEX, FLEX-2, SAFFIRE-I, SAFFIRE-II, SAFFIRE-III, SAME, SAME-R, SLICE, SPICE, plus PSI-funded ground reanalyses.
- **Date range:** from Space Shuttle-era candle experiments (Candle Flame in Microgravity, USML-1 on Columbia, June 1992), through Mir-era work (the 1997 Mir oxygen-generator fire is the canonical cautionary case), the long ISS era (2001–present), the SAFFIRE cargo-vehicle series (2016–2024, SAFFIRE VI being the last), to the upcoming FM2 lunar experiment. NASA Glenn's FM2 page calls it "the first-ever combustion experiment to be performed on another planetary body," investigating burning "on the Moon at its 1/6 reduced gravity level," and NASA reports it is "currently targeting a launch in late 2026."

**Volume and format heterogeneity within single investigations:**
- **FLEX:** NASA NTRS report 20150023456, *"Detailed Results from the Flame Extinguishment Experiment (FLEX) March 2009 to December 2011"* (Dietrich, Ferkul & Bryg), "presents the results of the first 284 tests performed. The intent is not to interpret the experimental results but rather to make them available to the entire scientific community for possible future interpretations" — i.e., raw dumps left for others to reconcile.
- **BASS-II:** "consists of 100 fuel samples"; the facility "produced 70 conference and journal articles on microgravity solid fuel combustion to date."
- **SAFFIRE-I:** per AmericaSpace (11 May 2020), it "marked the largest in-space fire experiment of its type and a total of 25 gigabits of data, including 40,989 images and data files were transmitted to investigators."
- **ACME's Burning Rate Emulator:** "Analysis of 59 BRE burn tests."

Formats include PDFs, peer-reviewed journal articles (Combustion and Flame, Proceedings of the Combustion Institute, Fire Technology), conference proceedings (International Conference on Environmental Systems), NASA Technical Memoranda (e.g., NASA-TM-202110011385, the BASS-II Summary Report), raw images/video, thermocouple traces, and PSI records with DOIs — none sharing a common schema.

**The literal manual process, from the sources:**
- Liao's grant reports the pull-categorize-process loop: literature review, then categorization of PSI data, then a bespoke MATLAB code to process it — because the data are not comparison-ready.
- The Decadal white paper (Liao et al.) states most past experiments "relied on camera images for detecting the flame regions" whose limits "pertained to the camera capabilities and settings," and concludes "these limitation[s] prevent direct comparisons between different experiments."
- Comparison papers (e.g., Bhattacharjee et al. 2016; Urban et al. 2019, *"Flame spread: Effects of microgravity and scale"*) are themselves the manual side-by-side exercise, each pulling "data for thick PMMA (from literature)" to place a new run against old ones.

### Layer 3 — Root cause, not symptom

Three structural causes, each with citable evidence:

**(a) Data isn't standardized across missions run decades apart by different teams.** The Decadal Survey white paper (Liao et al., *"Challenges and Research Needs for Micro- and Partial-Gravity Fires"*) states diagnostics "were limited," that most experiments "relied on camera images," and that such limitations "prevent direct comparisons between different experiments." Missions used different facilities (2.2s and 5.18s drop towers, parabolic flight, the Microgravity Science Glovebox, the Combustion Integrated Rack, and Cygnus cargo vehicles), different fuels (idealized PMMA and SIBAL cotton/fiberglass versus real Nomex/Ultem/Mylar), and different pressure/oxygen ranges (BASS limited to 1 atm and <22% O₂ by MSG constraints; SoFIE/CIR extending to 34% O₂). **Confirmed.**

**(b) Findings live in narrative PDFs, papers, and proceedings rather than structured data.** The FLEX report's stated purpose is to "make them available … for possible future interpretations," not to structure them. The Decadal white paper says that "in addition to the published data in the literature," a more comprehensive database "could allow more researchers to access the limited low gravity experiments" — acknowledging the corpus is currently literature-bound. **Confirmed.**

**(c) No one funded a unification effort; each mission was its own line item.** PSI was created in 2014 as an open-science archive to which PI teams submit their own datasets after each investigation closes; it was not a program to retroactively standardize or unify. The Decadal white paper states bluntly: "the current version of the PSI is far from completion. Combustion investigations before the 2000s are missing." NASA's PSI research-opportunity page frames reanalysis as something individual PIs must competitively propose ("find new ways to analyze the existing data … cross-referencing with other physical sciences database[s]"), i.e., unification is pushed onto single funded PIs rather than run centrally. **Confirmed structurally.**

### Layer 4 — Cost of leaving it unsolved

**Sourced consequences:**
- **Design decisions forced onto 1-g understanding.** NASA NTRS 20240002981, *"Preliminary Results from the Saffire VI Experiment"* (Urban et al., ICES 2024): "The lack of this experimental data has forced spacecraft designers to base their designs and safety precautions on 1-g understanding of flame spread, flame self-extinguishment, fire detection, and suppression." *(Note: this refers specifically to the absence of large-scale flight data, a related but distinct problem from data fragmentation; it is nonetheless the clearest sourced statement linking missing/inaccessible data to design risk.)*
- **Certification may be non-conservative.** Fletcher Miller et al., Decadal Survey topical white paper *"Spacecraft Materials Fire Safety"* (2023): "over the last decade, materials flammability testing in actual low gravity and partial gravity conditions has shown that this is sometimes not the case, at least for materials preferred by fire researchers, which notably are often different than the actual materials used in spacecraft." The paper's Table 1 shows the 1-g-vs-low-g oxygen-limit differences "are largest for the Lunar g level, indicating poor safety margins," and warns that new exploration atmospheres "of up to 34% oxygen will likely require the retest of many materials even under the current protocols." The Gollner et al. Decadal paper adds that Lunar and Martian gravity levels "could actually enhance the propensity for concurrent-flow flame spread."
- **Barrier to model validation and modern analysis.** The Decadal white paper states that applications of machine learning "to microgravity are rare and require an intense literature review of the available data," and that a comprehensive database "would accelerate model and theory development." This ties fragmentation directly to slower predictive-safety progress.
- **Duplication risk — flagged as INFERRED, not directly sourced.** I did not find a NASA document explicitly stating an experiment was re-run because a prior test could not be found. The sourced material supports the weaker claim that assembling prior data is onerous and that PIs must "leverage and extend" existing datasets manually; the stronger "duplicated experiment" cost is a reasonable inference but is **not** directly sourced and should not be overstated.

### Layer 5 — Prior attempts and where they stop

Partial fixes exist; each has a clear boundary line — and that boundary is the current edge of the unsolved problem:

- **Fujita, *"Solid combustion research in microgravity as a basis of fire safety in space"* (Proceedings of the Combustion Institute, 2015).** A widely-cited review synthesizing solid-combustion microgravity work. *Boundary:* it is a narrative review by one group, current only to 2015 (pre-SAFFIRE results, pre-ACME completion), and does not provide a unified, queryable comparison of the underlying datasets.
- **Individual comparison papers** — Bhattacharjee et al. (2016) opposed-flow comparison; Urban et al. (2019) *"Flame spread: Effects of microgravity and scale."* *Boundary:* each addresses one narrow phenomenon and manually pulls literature values; none spans the full BASS/FLEX/SAFFIRE/ACME corpus.
- **The BASS-II Summary Report (NASA-TM-202110011385, 2021) and the FLEX Detailed Results report.** *Boundary:* each documents a single investigation and explicitly leaves interpretation and comparison to others.
- **Liao's PSI grant (Task Book id=17602)** — the current live edge: a MATLAB pipeline to categorize and compare BASS, BASS-II, and SAFFIRE-I/II/III (with ISS Confined Combustion "considered"). *Boundary — where the unsolved problem still begins:* it is one competitively-funded PI; it covers only a subset of ISS-era solid-fuel investigations; it explicitly excludes pre-2000 combustion experiments (which the Decadal paper confirms are "missing" from PSI); it does not cover droplet/gaseous investigations (FLEX droplets, ACME, CFI); and it remains a manual/semi-automated processing effort rather than a standing capability.
- **The funded-grant landscape confirms no synthesis exists.** Among all funded combustion PSI grants, Liao's is essentially the only cross-investigation reanalysis; the others each reanalyze a **single** investigation — Yiguang Ju (Princeton) and Tanvir Farouk (South Carolina) on FLEX; Sean Garrick (Minnesota) on SAME; Fumiaki Takahashi (CWRU) on SLICE; Thomas Avedisian (Cornell) on FLEX; Mitchell Smooke (Yale) on SPICE; Paul DesJardin (SUNY) on BASS; Robert Pitz (Vanderbilt) on FLEX; Stephen Tse (Rutgers) on ACME S-Flames. This confirms that no funded effort synthesizes the full corpus.

## Recommendations
*(Problem-framing next steps only — deliberately no tool, platform, or technical solution.)*

1. **Treat this as a documented, owned problem, not an abstraction.** Anchor any future scoping on the named holders — Liao's grant as the working example of the manual process, and NASA-STD-6001B material engineers as the downstream decision-makers. *Threshold that would lower priority:* if PSI's own combustion holdings were completed back through the 1990s and given a common schema, the "hard to find/compare" symptom would materially shrink.
2. **Quantify the manual cost directly.** Interview the PIs named here (Liao, Olson, Ferkul) to measure hours spent assembling comparisons per paper; this is currently undocumented and is the single missing hard number that would size the problem.
3. **Separate the two conflated problems.** "Missing large-scale flight data" (the SAFFIRE-VI framing) and "fragmented/inaccessible existing data" (the PSI framing) are distinct; conflating them overstates what a data-access fix alone could solve. *Threshold:* only the second is addressable without flying new experiments.
4. **Prioritize by decision risk.** The Lunar-gravity flammability margins flagged as "poor" in the Decadal materials paper — feeding Artemis material selection and the FM2 late-2026 experiment — are the highest-consequence place the fragmentation bites, and where documenting the problem carries the most urgency.

## Caveats
- **Investigation counts are approximate.** PSI's combustion list is being added to "daily," and grouping choices (e.g., ACME's several sub-experiments; SAFFIRE I–VI, of which only I–III are individually catalogued in PSI) change the total. Treat "~20 combustion investigations" and "72 total" as accurate to the cited sources but not a fixed census.
- **The strongest downstream-cost quote (SAFFIRE VI) concerns the absence of large-scale flight data specifically, not fragmentation of existing archives.** I have flagged this distinction throughout rather than blending the two, to avoid overselling the connection.
- **The "duplicated experiments" cost is inferred, not directly sourced.**
- **Task Book pages id=17602 and id=17437 returned access errors on direct fetch**; their content was recovered via indexed cached text and cross-checked against NASA's official PSI awarded-research list and the Decadal white papers. Verbatim quotes from those pages should be treated as high-confidence but not visually confirmed against the live PDF.
- **Several forward-looking items are explicitly predictive/planned, not completed results** — the FM2 launch "late 2026" and predictions that Lunar gravity "could be more hazardous" or "could actually enhance" flame spread are stated in the sources as expectations, not established outcomes.
